🏛️ C2M2 Framework Overview

Cybersecurity Capability Maturity Model — Version 2.1

What is C2M2?

The Cybersecurity Capability Maturity Model (C2M2) is a voluntary framework developed by the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) to help organizations evaluate and improve their cybersecurity capabilities. It provides a structured way to measure the maturity of cybersecurity practices across ten domains using a three-level maturity scale.

C2M2 is designed to be sector-agnostic but is especially relevant to electricity, oil and natural gas, and other critical infrastructure sectors. Unlike compliance frameworks that define a binary pass/fail, C2M2 acknowledges that cybersecurity is a continuous journey and helps organizations identify where they are and prioritize where to go next.

Electric Utilities
Power Generation, Transmission & Distribution

Electric utilities — including investor-owned utilities (IOUs), municipal utilities, rural electric cooperatives, and independent power producers — generate, transmit, and distribute electricity to residential, commercial, and industrial customers. The U.S. bulk electric system is one of the most complex and interdependent engineered systems in the world, spanning thousands of generating stations, high-voltage transmission lines, substations, and millions of distribution endpoints.

Because nearly every other critical infrastructure sector depends on reliable electrical power, utilities represent a top-tier target for nation-state actors, ransomware groups, and hacktivists. The convergence of legacy Operational Technology (OT) — such as SCADA systems, energy management systems (EMS), and industrial control systems — with modern IT networks creates a particularly complex attack surface.

Key Cybersecurity Threats

  • Nation-state intrusions targeting grid control systems
  • Ransomware disrupting billing and operational systems
  • Supply chain attacks on SCADA/ICS vendors
  • Insider threats at remote substations
  • Physical-cyber coordinated attacks
  • Attacks on smart meter (AMI) infrastructure

Governing Regulations

  • NERC CIP (Critical Infrastructure Protection) Standards
  • FERC orders governing bulk electric system security
  • TSA Pipeline Security Directives (for dual-fuel utilities)
  • State PUC cybersecurity requirements
  • NIST SP 800-82 (ICS Security Guide)
C2M2 Relevance: Electric utilities were the original target audience for C2M2 (ES-C2M2, 2012). The framework's ASSET, ARCHITECTURE, and SITUATION domains directly map to managing IT/OT convergence and achieving NERC CIP compliance. C2M2 self-evaluations help utilities benchmark their maturity and justify cybersecurity investment decisions to regulators and boards.

Oil & Natural Gas
Upstream, Midstream & Downstream Operations

The oil and natural gas (ONG) sector encompasses the full energy supply chain — from upstream exploration and production (drilling, extraction) to midstream transportation (pipelines, compressor stations, LNG terminals) and downstream refining, processing, and distribution. This sector is foundational to national energy security and underpins fuel supply for transportation, heating, and industrial production across the country.

ONG infrastructure relies heavily on geographically dispersed industrial control systems (ICS), pipeline supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) networks, and remote terminal units (RTUs). The 2021 Colonial Pipeline ransomware attack — which disrupted fuel supplies across the U.S. Southeast — highlighted the catastrophic potential of cyberattacks on this sector.

Key Cybersecurity Threats

  • Ransomware targeting pipeline SCADA and billing systems
  • Nation-state APT intrusions seeking persistent access
  • Remote access exploitation of unmanned facilities
  • Attacks on LNG terminal control systems
  • Third-party vendor compromise (supply chain)
  • Insider sabotage at remote pump/compressor stations

Governing Regulations

  • TSA Pipeline Security Directives (SD-02C, SD-02D)
  • DOT PHMSA pipeline safety regulations
  • API Cybersecurity Standards (API 1164)
  • CISA Cross-Sector Cybersecurity Performance Goals
  • ONG-C2M2 sector-specific supplement
C2M2 Relevance: DOE published the ONG-C2M2 in 2014 specifically for this sector. The THIRD-PARTIES, RESPONSE, and RISK domains are especially critical for pipeline operators managing dispersed infrastructure and complex contractor ecosystems. C2M2 aligns closely with TSA Pipeline Security Directive requirements.

Water Systems
Drinking Water & Wastewater Treatment Infrastructure

The water and wastewater sector includes over 148,000 public water systems and roughly 16,000 publicly owned wastewater treatment works across the United States. These systems collect, treat, and distribute safe drinking water while managing wastewater disposal — functions that are directly linked to public health. Water utilities range from large metropolitan authorities serving millions of customers to small rural systems serving a few hundred people.

Water systems often rely on aging SCADA and industrial control systems to automate treatment processes including chemical dosing, pump operations, and disinfection. The 2021 Oldsmar, Florida incident — where an attacker remotely accessed a water treatment plant and attempted to alter sodium hydroxide levels — demonstrated the life-safety implications of inadequate cybersecurity in this sector.

Key Cybersecurity Threats

  • Remote manipulation of treatment chemical levels
  • Ransomware disrupting billing and operational systems
  • Exploitation of internet-exposed HMI/SCADA interfaces
  • Insider threats with physical access to treatment facilities
  • Attacks on sensor networks and water quality monitoring
  • Supply chain compromise of ICS vendors

Governing Regulations

  • America's Water Infrastructure Act (AWIA) — risk assessments
  • EPA Cybersecurity Guidance for Water Utilities
  • CISA Water Sector Security Guidance
  • Safe Drinking Water Act (SDWA) requirements
  • WaterISAC Threat Intelligence Sharing
C2M2 Relevance: Water utilities — many resource-constrained — benefit from C2M2's scalable, non-prescriptive structure. The ASSET and THREAT domains help utilities inventory aging ICS assets and prioritize limited cybersecurity investments. C2M2 supports EPA's requirement that water systems conduct cybersecurity risk and resilience assessments.

Industrial / Operational Technology (OT)
Manufacturing, Process Control & Industrial Automation

Industrial and Operational Technology (OT) environments span a vast range of sectors — including manufacturing, chemical processing, aerospace, food & beverage, pharmaceuticals, automotive, and defense industrial base. OT refers to hardware and software that monitors and controls physical equipment, processes, and events. This includes Programmable Logic Controllers (PLCs), Distributed Control Systems (DCS), Human Machine Interfaces (HMIs), and Industrial IoT (IIoT) devices.

The convergence of IT and OT networks — driven by Industry 4.0, smart manufacturing, and remote monitoring — has dramatically expanded the attack surface of industrial environments. Disruption of OT systems can cause production outages, equipment damage, environmental incidents, and in some cases, physical harm to workers. Adversaries increasingly target OT specifically to cause cascading real-world effects.

Key Cybersecurity Threats

  • ICS-targeted malware (e.g., INDUSTROYER, TRITON/TRISIS)
  • Ransomware halting production lines
  • Exploitation of legacy PLCs and unpatched firmware
  • Remote access abuse via VPN and jump servers
  • Supply chain attacks on OT hardware and software vendors
  • Intellectual property theft from engineering systems

Governing Standards

  • IEC 62443 (Industrial Cybersecurity Standard)
  • NIST SP 800-82 Rev. 3 (ICS Security Guide)
  • CISA ICS-CERT advisories and guidance
  • CMMC (for Defense Industrial Base manufacturers)
  • ISA/IEC 62443 Zones and Conduits model
C2M2 Relevance: The C2M2 was explicitly designed to address both IT and OT asset environments. The ARCHITECTURE domain guides network segmentation between IT and OT zones, while the ASSET domain addresses ICS/SCADA inventory. C2M2's MIL progression allows manufacturers to build maturity incrementally without disrupting production operations.

Healthcare
Hospitals, Health Systems & Medical Infrastructure

The healthcare sector includes hospitals, integrated health systems, physician practices, outpatient clinics, long-term care facilities, diagnostic labs, and medical device manufacturers. Healthcare organizations manage enormous volumes of protected health information (PHI) while operating life-critical systems including electronic health records (EHRs), clinical decision support systems, medical imaging systems (PACS/DICOM), and networked medical devices such as infusion pumps, ventilators, and patient monitors.

Healthcare is consistently ranked among the most-targeted sectors for cyberattacks. Ransomware attacks on hospitals have directly delayed care and been linked to patient harm. The sector faces a unique dual challenge: balancing robust security with the need for rapid, uninterrupted access to patient data during clinical care.

Key Cybersecurity Threats

  • Ransomware encrypting EHR systems and disrupting care
  • Medical device exploitation (infusion pumps, PACS)
  • Phishing targeting clinical staff credentials
  • Business Email Compromise (BEC) for financial fraud
  • Third-party vendor breaches (billing, telehealth)
  • Data theft of PHI for identity fraud and extortion

Governing Regulations

  • HIPAA Security Rule (45 CFR Parts 160 & 164)
  • HHS 405(d) Cybersecurity Practices for Healthcare
  • FDA Pre/Post-market Medical Device Cybersecurity Guidance
  • State data breach notification laws
  • HICP (Health Industry Cybersecurity Practices)
C2M2 Relevance: Healthcare organizations leverage C2M2's WORKFORCE, ACCESS, and RESPONSE domains to address insider threats, privileged access to EHR systems, and ransomware incident response. C2M2's maturity-based approach complements HIPAA Security Rule compliance by providing a structured path to improving security posture beyond minimum regulatory requirements.

Financial Services
Banking, Insurance, Investment & Payment Systems

The financial services sector encompasses commercial and investment banks, credit unions, insurance companies, securities exchanges, payment processors, fintech firms, and clearinghouses. Financial institutions serve as the backbone of economic activity, holding consumer assets, facilitating transactions, and providing credit. The sector operates vast interconnected technology ecosystems including core banking platforms, trading systems, payment networks (ACH, SWIFT, Fedwire), and mobile banking applications used by hundreds of millions of customers.

The financial sector is among the most heavily regulated and best-resourced for cybersecurity — yet it remains a top target due to the direct monetary value of its systems. Sophisticated adversaries pursue wire fraud, account takeover, market manipulation, and systemic disruption. A successful attack on a major clearinghouse or payment processor could cascade across the entire economy.

Key Cybersecurity Threats

  • Nation-state attacks targeting SWIFT and interbank systems
  • Ransomware disrupting banking operations
  • Credential stuffing and account takeover at scale
  • Business Email Compromise (BEC) wire fraud
  • Insider trading via unauthorized system access
  • Third-party fintech and processor supply chain risk

Governing Regulations

  • GLBA Safeguards Rule (FTC / OCC)
  • FFIEC Cybersecurity Assessment Tool (CAT)
  • SEC Cybersecurity Disclosure Rules (2023)
  • NY DFS Part 500 Cybersecurity Regulation
  • PCI DSS (Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard)
C2M2 Relevance: Financial institutions use C2M2's RISK, THIRD-PARTIES, and PROGRAM domains to manage vendor risk, demonstrate governance maturity to regulators, and benchmark against peers. C2M2 complements the FFIEC CAT — both frameworks share a maturity-based approach and can be cross-walked to support comprehensive cyber risk reporting to boards and examiners.

Government
Federal, State, Local & Tribal Agencies

Government entities at all levels — federal departments and agencies, state governments, county and municipal governments, tribal nations, and public authorities — rely on technology systems to deliver essential public services including benefits administration, law enforcement, tax collection, permitting, emergency management, and public communications. Government IT environments are diverse, often legacy-laden, and span both classified and unclassified networks.

Government agencies are prime targets for nation-state espionage, hacktivism, and ransomware. The SolarWinds supply chain compromise (2020) and the attack on the U.S. Office of Personnel Management (2015) — which exposed records of over 21 million federal employees — illustrate the scale of risk. State and local governments face particular challenges due to limited IT staffing and budget constraints.

Key Cybersecurity Threats

  • Nation-state espionage targeting classified/sensitive systems
  • Supply chain compromise of government IT vendors
  • Ransomware targeting municipal and county systems
  • Phishing targeting government employee credentials
  • Election infrastructure interference
  • Insider threats with privileged access to sensitive data

Governing Frameworks

  • FISMA (Federal Information Security Modernization Act)
  • NIST RMF (Risk Management Framework, SP 800-37)
  • CISA Binding Operational Directives (BODs)
  • FedRAMP (Cloud Authorization Framework)
  • Executive Order 14028 (Improving Cybersecurity)
C2M2 Relevance: Government agencies apply C2M2's PROGRAM and RISK domains to align cybersecurity strategy with mission requirements and demonstrate security governance to oversight bodies. The WORKFORCE domain supports agencies in building internal cybersecurity capacity. C2M2 self-evaluations can complement FISMA reporting and NIST RMF authorization activities.

Research Institutions
Universities, National Labs & R&D Organizations

Research institutions — including universities, federally funded research and development centers (FFRDCs), national laboratories (e.g., Oak Ridge, Argonne, Sandia), and private R&D organizations — generate and steward some of the nation's most valuable intellectual assets. These include cutting-edge research in defense technologies, biomedical sciences, artificial intelligence, quantum computing, and advanced energy systems. Research networks typically prioritize openness and collaboration, which can create tension with security requirements.

Research institutions present a unique cybersecurity challenge: they must protect proprietary research, classified data, and government-funded intellectual property while maintaining the open, collaborative environment essential to scientific progress. Nation-state actors — particularly from China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea — actively target universities and labs to steal research without the cost of development.

Key Cybersecurity Threats

  • Nation-state intellectual property theft campaigns
  • Phishing targeting researcher and student accounts
  • Compromise of research computing clusters (HPC)
  • Insider threats — researchers with foreign connections
  • Ransomware targeting administrative and research systems
  • Vulnerabilities in collaborative research tools and portals

Governing Frameworks

  • NIST SP 800-171 (CUI protection for federal contractors)
  • CMMC 2.0 (for DoD-funded research)
  • NSF Cybersecurity requirements for grantees
  • DOE Laboratory Cybersecurity Program standards
  • FERPA (for student records protection)
C2M2 Relevance: Research institutions — particularly those receiving federal funding from DOE, NSF, DoD, or NIH — use C2M2's ASSET, ACCESS, and THIRD-PARTIES domains to inventory research computing assets, control access to sensitive data, and manage collaborator risk. The framework's non-prescriptive, scalable design accommodates the diverse and decentralized nature of university and laboratory environments.

How C2M2 is Structured

C2M2 organizes cybersecurity activities into ten domains, each representing a functional area of cybersecurity management. Within each domain, practices are grouped into objectives and assigned to Maturity Indicator Levels (MIL 1–3).

Domain Structure

  • ASSET — Asset, Change, and Configuration Management
  • THREAT — Threat and Vulnerability Management
  • RISK — Risk Management
  • ACCESS — Identity and Access Management
  • SITUATION — Situational Awareness
  • RESPONSE — Event and Incident Response, Continuity of Operations
  • THIRD-PARTIES — Third-Party Risk Management
  • WORKFORCE — Workforce Management
  • ARCHITECTURE — Cybersecurity Architecture
  • PROGRAM — Cybersecurity Program Management

MIL Progression

Each domain can independently achieve MIL 0 (not performed) through MIL 3 (optimized). Achieving a MIL level in one domain does not require achieving the same level in other domains — organizations can have uneven maturity profiles.

Self-Evaluation vs. Independent Evaluation

Self-Evaluation

Organizations may conduct a C2M2 self-evaluation using the DOE-provided toolkits and facilitation guides. A self-evaluation involves subject matter experts across the organization rating their own implementation of practices. Results are internal and used to guide improvement planning.

Independent Evaluation

A third-party (independent) evaluation provides an objective assessment and may be required by sector regulators or contractual agreements. An independent evaluator reviews evidence, conducts interviews, and validates practice implementation against C2M2 criteria.

Key Differences

  • Self-evaluation is internal, voluntary, and lower cost
  • Independent evaluation is evidence-driven and externally validated
  • Neither evaluation results in a pass/fail — both produce a maturity profile
  • Evaluations are point-in-time; organizations should repeat them periodically

C2M2 Version History

2012
C2M2 v1.0 — Original model released by DOE targeting the electricity subsector.
2014
ES-C2M2 / ONG-C2M2 — Separate sector-specific supplements released for Electricity Subsector and Oil & Natural Gas subsector.
2019
C2M2 v1.1 — Minor revision with clarifications and alignment to NIST CSF updates.
2022
C2M2 v2.0 / v2.1 — Major revision consolidating sector supplements, expanding to 10 domains, adding Supply Chain and Architecture domains, and aligning with NIST CSF 1.1 and NERC CIP.
